Asheville Watchdog September 17, 2026 Update

HCA CEO Hazen Makes First Public Acknowledgment That Mission Hospital 'Needed to Do Better' in Closed-Door Asheville Meeting

HCA CEO Sam Hazen surprised attendees at an Aug. 28 closed meeting at the Mountain Area Health Education Center in Asheville — marking his first public acknowledgment that Mission Hospital has not always met patient and community expectations. Alongside a co-announced $32 million capital spending plan, Hazen framed HCA as a champion of rural healthcare but did not make public commitments on staffing ratios or compliance timelines. Advocates note the meeting was closed to the press and public, and that Hazen's prior claims of frequent Asheville visits were contradicted by 14 local elected officials.

How to position this
  • Hazen's first acknowledgment of Mission's failures came only after years of community pressure, four CMS Immediate Jeopardy citations, and an active AG lawsuit — not proactive leadership.
  • The meeting was closed to the public and press; HCA controlled the narrative without open community accountability.
  • Hazen claimed $750M in Mission investment since 2019, yet staffing ratios fell from 6.1 to 3.7 FTE/bed — well below the 5.1 NC average. Capital spending announcements do not address staffing.
  • 14 local elected officials previously told Asheville Watchdog they had never met Hazen — his appearance only came after formal county and city resolutions demanded it.

HCA CEO Sam Hazen attended a closed Aug. 28 meeting of the WNC Health Policy Initiative at MAHEC — the first time he publicly acknowledged Mission's performance gaps. He portrayed HCA as having invested nearly $750 million in Mission since 2019 and announced a $32 million facilities spending plan for WNC. State Sen. Julie Mayfield, attending virtually, said she was hopeful the appearance marked a new course but noted concerns remain. The meeting was not open to the public or press, limiting accountability.

KFF Health News / Washington Post August 9, 2026 Monopoly

National Investigation: Mission Hospital Charges Double Competitors — A 'Prepackaged Monopoly' Under HCA

A major investigation by Peter Whoriskey for KFF Health News and the Washington Post documented Mission Hospital's extreme commercial pricing under HCA: a knee replacement runs ~$40,000 at Mission vs. ~$16,000 at Catawba Valley; RAND 2024 data shows Mission charges 334% of Medicare vs. a 237% NC benchmark competitor and a 280% NC average. Economists and advocates trace the pricing power directly to Mission's 1998 merger monopoly, the 2015 repeal of state profit limits, and HCA's 2019 purchase of what one source called a 'prepackaged monopoly.' The story features Reclaim Healthcare WNC and Sen. Julie Mayfield.

How to position this
  • Mission charges 334% of Medicare for commercial services — 57 percentage points above the nearest NC benchmark competitor — a direct consequence of the monopoly HCA purchased and has exploited.
  • WNC employers and patients pay double or more for the same procedures available at other NC hospitals; HCA profits from monopoly pricing while Mission's patient experience rating sits at 2 stars.
  • The investigation names HCA's 2019 acquisition as the final step in converting a 'prepackaged monopoly' into a for-profit pricing machine at WNC's only major hospital system.
  • Reclaim Healthcare WNC and Sen. Mayfield are prominently featured — reinforcing that community advocates identified this pricing crisis and have been fighting it since HCA's arrival.

KFF Health News and the Washington Post published an August 9, 2026 investigation tracing Mission Hospital's pricing monopoly from a 1998 merger through HCA's 2019 acquisition. RAND data shows Mission's commercial prices at 334% of Medicare — well above the 237% rate of NC competitor Catawba Valley Medical Center and above the 280% NC average. Specific procedures showed dramatic price disparities: a knee replacement was roughly $40,000 at Mission vs. $16,000 at Catawba; a breast biopsy $7,500 vs. $1,700. Yale economist Zack Cooper, employer Katie Button, and Sen. Julie Mayfield of Reclaim Healthcare WNC are featured. The piece traces how the 2015 repeal of state profit limits and HCA's acquisition entrenched monopoly pricing.

NC DOJ / Blue Ridge Public Radio July 28, 2026 Lawsuit

Judge Denies HCA's Motion for Summary Judgment — AG Jackson's Mission Hospital Lawsuit Moves Forward

NC Business Court Judge Julianna Theall Earp issued a 55-page ruling on July 28, 2026, denying HCA's motion for summary judgment and allowing Attorney General Jeff Jackson's lawsuit against HCA over Mission Hospital to proceed. The court agreed the Asset Purchase Agreement requires HCA to actually provide emergency and cancer services, and rejected HCA's attempt to exclude expert testimony that Mission's ED staffing plan was too lean to provide adequate care. AG Jackson said: 'HCA threw every argument against the wall to try to get this case thrown out, but they didn't stick.'

How to position this
  • The court's ruling that HCA must actually 'provide' emergency and cancer services — not just maintain infrastructure — directly validates advocates' core argument about HCA's service cuts.
  • The court refused to exclude expert testimony that Mission's emergency department staffing plan was too lean to deliver safe care, reinforcing advocates' staffing ratio concerns.
  • HCA's 'every argument against the wall' strategy to kill the case before trial failed — the lawsuit proceeds on the core issues of service degradation and unsafe staffing.
  • This ruling advances the only legal mechanism that can force HCA to honor the commitments made when it acquired a community's nonprofit hospital for $1.5 billion.

On July 28, 2026, NC Business Court Judge Julianna Theall Earp denied HCA's motion for summary judgment, allowing the AG's lawsuit over Mission Hospital service cuts to proceed. The court agreed the APA obligates HCA to actually provide — not merely offer infrastructure for — emergency and cancer services. It also denied HCA's effort to exclude expert testimony that Mission's ED staffing plan was inadequate to provide safe care. HCA denied all allegations and says it is in compliance with the purchase agreement.

  • HCA's refusal to share data with the independent monitor is a transparency failure that harms the public's right to know how their former nonprofit hospital is being run.
  • The monitor noted the core factual issues in the AG litigation remain unchanged — meaning HCA has had years to fix identified problems and has not done so.

Mission faced a July 26 CMS deadline to demonstrate compliance under its Enhanced Plan of Correction. National Nurses United / Too Big to Care Evergreen Reference Safety

NNU 'Too Big to Care' HCA Case Study: Asheville Chapter Documents Monopoly, Staffing Collapse, and Aggressive Billing

National Nurses United's in-depth 'Too Big to Care' case study on HCA Healthcare includes a major Asheville/Mission section documenting the company's national playbook: market domination, price increases, service cuts, and aggressive bedside billing. Key Mission facts include HCA controlling 58% of Asheville discharges, trauma activation fees up to $50,000, 15 trauma nurses departing in 2020, and Mission nurses unionizing in September 2020 in the largest non-union Southern hospital win since 1975. This resource remains essential for advocates framing HCA's Asheville conduct within its national for-profit strategy.

  • Trauma activation fees up to $50,000 and bedside debt collection at Mission are features of HCA's national business model, not aberrations — the case study shows the same playbook across all HCA markets.
  • Mission nurses unionized in September 2020 in a historic organizing victory, a direct response to HCA's post-acquisition staffing cuts and working conditions.
  • The NNU case study situates WNC's experience within a national pattern of for-profit hospital monopolization — making Mission a bellwether for the country's broader hospital accountability crisis.

The NNU 'Too Big to Care' case study documents HCA's national anticompetitive playbook and devotes extensive coverage to Mission Hospital in Asheville. It traces HCA controlling roughly 58% of Asheville discharges, post-2019 service cuts, surprise billing, bedside debt collection, trauma activation fees reaching $50,000, and an exodus of 15 trauma nurses in 2020. Mission nurses unionized in September 2020 — the largest Southern non-union hospital organizing win since 1975. Antitrust class-action suits and HCA fraud settlement history are also documented.
