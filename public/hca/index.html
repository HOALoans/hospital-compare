Asheville Watchdog September 24, 2026 Lawsuit

Antitrust Class Action Against Mission and HCA Hits Major Roadblock

A North Carolina judge denied class certification in a private antitrust lawsuit accusing HCA of exploiting Mission's regional monopoly to inflate insurance premiums. The September 8 ruling blocks WNC residents and employers from proceeding together as a class — a significant procedural blow to civil accountability efforts. The AG's separate lawsuit continues to move forward.

How to position this
  • The antitrust case alleged HCA exploited the Mission monopoly — built through the 1998 nonprofit merger — to overcharge patients and insurers across western NC.
  • The class certification denial is a procedural setback, not an exoneration: HCA's pricing power and monopoly conduct remain central to the AG's ongoing lawsuit.
  • The AG's case survived HCA's motion to dismiss on July 28, 2026 — that accountability track is fully intact and advancing.
  • Community advocates should distinguish this private civil suit from the state AG lawsuit — both address the same underlying monopoly harm.

On September 8, 2026, Judge Mark A. Davis of the NC Superior Court denied class certification in Davis v. HCA Healthcare, a private antitrust suit filed in 2023 alleging that HCA used Mission's regional monopoly to drive up health insurance premiums. The court excluded key portions of plaintiffs' expert report as insufficient. The ruling blocks the case from proceeding as a class action, though individual claims may still advance. The NC Attorney General's separate lawsuit — which survived summary judgment July 28 — remains on track.

Asheville Watchdog / GovInfo September 9, 2026 Congress

Rep. Edwards Introduces 'HCA Mission Act' to Give CMS $10,000-Per-Day Penalty Power

Congressman Chuck Edwards (R-NC) introduced H.R. 10311, the Health Care Accountability Mission Act of 2026, to amend the Social Security Act and allow CMS to impose a $10,000-per-day civil monetary penalty on hospitals that repeatedly jeopardize patient safety. The bill — referred to the House Ways and Means Committee — has no co-sponsors yet but represents the first Mission-specific federal legislation targeting HCA's pattern of Immediate Jeopardy citations. It follows HCA CEO Sam Hazen's April 2026 Ways and Means testimony, which urged eliminating CON laws but made no mention of Mission's four IJ citations.

How to position this
  • HCA's Mission Hospital has received four Immediate Jeopardy citations (2021, 2024, 2025, 2026) — each the most serious patient-safety finding CMS can issue — yet faced no financial penalty under current law.
  • H.R. 10311 would close the regulatory gap: a $10,000/day penalty would create real financial consequence for persistent noncompliance rather than just the threat of Medicare termination.
  • HCA CEO Hazen testified before Ways and Means in April 2026 urging CON elimination — but his written testimony did not address Mission's IJ citations, staffing collapse, or federal noncompliance.
  • Advocates should call on Ways and Means members to co-sponsor H.R. 10311 and ask HCA why it opposes financial penalties for patient-safety failures.

H.R. 10311, the Health Care Accountability Mission Act of 2026, was introduced by Rep. Chuck Edwards on September 8, 2026, and referred to the House Ways and Means Committee. The bill would amend Title XVIII of the Social Security Act to establish a civil monetary penalty of up to $10,000 per day for hospitals found to have repeatedly failed to meet federal patient safety standards — directly targeting the regulatory gap exposed by Mission Hospital's four Immediate Jeopardy designations since 2019. Currently, CMS can only terminate a hospital's Medicare/Medicaid agreement; it cannot levy ongoing financial penalties. The bill currently has no co-sponsors.

KFF Health News / Washington Post August 9, 2026 Monopoly

Major Investigation: Mission Hospital Charges Twice the Price for Same Procedures — Monopoly Markup Documented

A landmark KFF Health News and Washington Post investigation by Peter Whoriskey reveals Mission Hospital charges dramatically more than comparable NC hospitals under the same insurance plans: a knee replacement runs ~$40,000 at Mission vs. ~$16,000 at Catawba Valley Medical Center; a breast biopsy costs UnitedHealthcare $7,500 at Mission vs. $1,700 at Catawba. RAND 2024 data shows Mission charges 334% of Medicare — far above NC's 280% benchmark. Economists and Sen. Julie Mayfield tie the pricing power directly to HCA acquiring a 'prepackaged monopoly' built from Mission's 1998 nonprofit merger.

How to position this
  • Mission charges 334% of Medicare on commercial plans vs. a 280% NC average — HCA is extracting a monopoly premium that falls on WNC patients, employers, and taxpayers.
  • The same knee replacement costs ~$40,000 at Mission and ~$16,000 at Catawba Valley under the same Blue Cross plan — price differences driven by market power, not care quality.
  • HCA purchased Mission knowing it was acquiring a regional monopoly — and then cut staff, earned 2-star HCAHPS ratings, and raised prices. That is the HCA business model.
  • This investigation is essential reading for employers, legislators, and patients in WNC: share it and ask HCA why Mission's prices are so far above the state average.

Published August 9, 2026, this major investigation documents how Mission Hospital's regional monopoly — traced to a 1998 nonprofit merger and amplified by HCA's 2019 purchase — enables prices roughly double those of comparable NC hospitals. RAND 2024 data puts Mission's commercial prices at 334% of Medicare vs. 237% at Catawba Valley and a 280% NC benchmark. The piece features patient Marcelle Crago, employer/chef Katie Button, Yale economist Zack Cooper, and Sen. Julie Mayfield of Reclaim Healthcare WNC. The investigation found the 2015 repeal of NC's hospital profit limits and HCA's acquisition created a 'prepackaged monopoly' with unchecked pricing power in WNC.
