Washington Post / KFF Health News August 9, 2026 Monopoly

Washington Post / KFF: Mission Charges ~$40K for Knee Replacement vs. ~$16K an Hour Away — Monopoly Pricing Goes National

A major Washington Post / KFF Health News investigation uses newly disclosed hospital price data to show how Mission's monopoly power drives costs: under the same Blue Cross plan, a knee replacement was about $40,000 at Mission Hospital versus roughly $16,000 at Catawba Valley Medical Center in Hickory. Rand data put Mission's 2024 commercial prices at 334% of Medicare (vs. 237% at Catawba and a 280% state benchmark). The piece centers Asheville as a national cautionary tale — and quotes Sen. Julie Mayfield on Reclaim Healthcare WNC's work holding HCA accountable after staffing cuts, physician flight, and repeated Immediate Jeopardy findings.

How to position this
  • Hard price proof, not anecdote: Same insurer, same procedure — Mission ~$40K vs. Catawba ~$16K — puts monopoly overcharging in black-and-white numbers anyone can repeat.
  • Everyone pays, not just patients: Higher hospital prices get passed through insurance premiums; Asheville employers and families absorb Mission's monopoly markup whether or not they use the hospital.
  • HCA inherited a prepackaged monopoly: The 1998 merger created Mission's market power; after profit caps were dropped in 2015, HCA bought that monopoly in 2019 — and prices remain far above peers.
  • National spotlight on Reclaim's case: WaPo/KFF names Reclaim Healthcare WNC and Mayfield's warning that unregulated hospital monopolies harm the public — use this coverage to press legislators and regulators.

Peter Whoriskey's August 9, 2026 Washington Post / KFF Health News investigation shows how hospital mergers inflate prices, with Mission Hospital as the lead example. Serif Health data found a Blue Cross knee replacement cost about $40,000 at Mission versus roughly $16,000 at Catawba Valley Medical Center. Other Mission–Catawba gaps include breast biopsy ($7,500 vs. $1,700) and hernia repair ($17,700 vs. $9,600) under UnitedHealthcare. Rand figures put Mission's 2024 commercial prices at 334% of Medicare. The article traces Mission's 1998 merger monopoly, the 2015 repeal of state profit limits, and HCA's 2019 purchase, and features patient Marcelle Crago, employer Katie Button, Yale economist Zack Cooper, and Sen. Julie Mayfield of Reclaim Healthcare WNC.

WLOS August 5, 2026 Monitor

Independent Monitor: HCA Contractually Barred From Selling Mission for 10 Years — and Noncompliance Findings Stand

Following the August 4 community Q&A webinar, monitor representative Rachel Ryan told WLOS that under the 2019 Asset Purchase Agreement HCA cannot sell Mission Hospital during the first decade of ownership without advisory board consent — and said the monitor is unaware of any HCA desire to sell. Ryan reiterated the 2025 monitoring report's two areas of potential noncompliance: service discontinuation and Mission's four Immediate Jeopardy designations. The monitor's findings have been forwarded to the NC Attorney General, whose lawsuit against HCA is now advancing.

How to position this
  • APA locks HCA in: HCA cannot simply exit or sell Mission — it must fix the hospital it broke, making accountability legally enforceable for years to come.
  • Service cuts as contract breach: The monitor's finding that industrial rehab went to 'zero caseload' and was functionally discontinued is a concrete potential APA violation — exactly what HCA promised not to do.
  • Four Immediate Jeopardy citations in five years: The monitor has flagged repeated IJ designations as a potential noncompliance area, reinforcing that Mission's safety failures are a pattern, not isolated incidents.
  • Monitor data obstruction: The monitor publicly acknowledged prior difficulties getting information from HCA — a pattern of non-cooperation that advocates should highlight as undermining accountability.

After Dogwood Health Trust and Affiliated Monitors hosted a public Q&A webinar on August 4, monitor general counsel Rachel Ryan confirmed to WLOS that the APA obligates HCA not to sell Mission Hospital during its first 10 years of ownership and that any sale would require advisory board consent. Ryan restated that the 2025 monitoring report identified two areas of potential noncompliance: HCA's decision to discontinue certain services (including industrial rehabilitation at CarePartners) and Mission's repeated Immediate Jeopardy designations. The monitor's concerns have been transmitted to the NC Attorney General's Office for review. Ryan noted that HCA had previously hampered data-sharing with the monitor, though HCA has since agreed to improved processes.

NC Department of Justice July 28, 2026 Lawsuit

Judge Denies HCA's Motion to Dismiss: AG Lawsuit Against Mission Hospital Proceeds

NC Business Court Judge Julianna Earp denied most of HCA's motion for summary judgment on July 28, ruling that all claims in Attorney General Jeff Jackson's lawsuit will proceed. The court agreed with the AG that the 2019 purchase agreement requires HCA to take all steps necessary to actually provide emergency and cancer services — not merely to make them nominally available. HCA's bid to have the case thrown out before trial failed across all major arguments.

How to position this
  • HCA's legal shield failed: Every major argument HCA threw at the court was rejected — the case now proceeds and HCA faces a full trial on its broken acquisition promises.
  • Court defines 'providing services' broadly: The judge's ruling that HCA must 'actually provide' services — not merely list them — sets a meaningful legal standard for all future enforcement of hospital acquisition agreements in NC.
  • Seven years of broken promises on trial: The lawsuit covers HCA's failure to maintain emergency and cancer care since the 2019 acquisition — a record of harm that WNC residents have lived.
  • Pressure multiplies: With the AG lawsuit advancing, Dogwood's noncompliance referral, and active federal monitoring all simultaneous, HCA faces accountability on three fronts at once.

On July 28, 2026, NC Business Court Judge Julianna Earp denied most of HCA's motion for summary judgment in Attorney General Jeff Jackson's lawsuit over Mission Hospital's emergency and cancer care failures. The court ruled that the HCA asset purchase agreement requires HCA to actually provide those services, not just nominally offer them — agreeing with the AG on a key legal interpretation. All claims, including those related to emergency department staffing and patient care, will proceed. The one narrow exception: the court found the AG had not produced sufficient evidence of a missing ENT surgeon in the ED as required for Level 2 trauma designation.
